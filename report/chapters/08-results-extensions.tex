\chapter{Results: Extensions Beyond the Paper}\label{ch:results-ext}

None of the experiments in this chapter is in the paper. Each section restates its question, gives the complete

\section{Extension 2: best-of-4 sampling and DPO}\label{sec:ext2}

\textbf{Question.} TS keeps one greedy pair per question and discards the rest. Does sampling more candidates recover
validated data? Can rejected programs serve as negatives?

\input{generated/ext2_labels.tex}

\begin{observation}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{More validated data.} Sampling three more candidates per question raises the number of questions with a
validated program by 52--62\% (\cref{tab:gen-ext2-labels}). For example, seed~1 goes from 426 to 673.
\item \textbf{TS's own pair is sometimes wrong.} For 75, 89 and 73 questions (seeds 1--3), a sampled program
validated while TS's greedy SQL gives different rows. These greedy programs became DPO negatives.
\item \textbf{DPO pairs.} 397--438 pairs per seed. About three quarters have a rejected program that runs but returns
different rows.
\end{itemize}
\end{observation}

\input{generated/ext2_scores.tex}

\begin{observation}
Round-1 \Mviii{} over 3 seeds:
\begin{itemize}
\item TS 0.5266, bestof4 0.5330 ($+0.0064$), DPO 0.5057 ($-0.0209$, $p<0.0001$), NV 0.5401.
\item bestof4's gain is not stable across seeds: $+0.0065$, $+0.0282$, $-0.0156$.
\item DPO is below bestof4 on every seed: 1 cell higher and 7 lower on seed~1, 2/6 on seed~2, 0/8 on seed~3.
\end{itemize}
DPO multiplies invalid SQL. Compile or execution errors from bestof4 to DPO, zero-shot:
\begin{itemize}
\item WikiTQ: 15 $\to$ 58 (seed~1) and 17 $\to$ 55 (seed~2);
\item BIRD: 9 $\to$ 11 and 9 $\to$ 28.
\end{itemize}
Training ended with reward margins of 3.40, 5.35 and 3.19, and reward accuracy of 0.98--1.00, on 397--438 pairs over 2
epochs.
\end{observation}

\input{generated/dpo_dev.tex}

\begin{observation}
\begin{itemize}
\item Gentler DPO (lower learning rate or one epoch: v1, v2, v3, v5) removes most of the damage on dev:
$-0.0018$ to $-0.0061$ against bestof4, compared with $-0.0208$ for the first settings.
\item Raising $\beta$ to 0.5 at the original learning rate (v4) does not help ($-0.0244$), and ends with the same
reward margin (3.40). The learning rate, not $\beta$, drives the loss.
\item The differences among v1--v3 (at most 0.0034) are smaller than one question in 300.
\item No variant exceeds bestof4's dev mean (best: v2, 0.5600 against 0.5618), so under the rule fixed beforehand DPO
was not re-tested on the test sets.
\end{itemize}
\end{observation}

\begin{interpretation}
DPO's large reward margins and near-perfect reward accuracy show that the policy separated chosen from rejected
programs easily, and moved far from the reference in doing so. The SFT term did not prevent it.

The rejected programs are mostly \emph{plausible} SQL that returns different rows. Pushing their likelihood down also
pushes down nearby correct SQL; the rise in execution errors is consistent with this, but was not tested directly.

Best-of-4's small gain, despite 52--62\% more data, suggests that the extra validated programs are mostly easy
questions the model already answers.
\end{interpretation}

\section{Extension 3: repair-TS}\label{sec:ext-repair}

\textbf{Question.} Can rejected pairs be recovered by one execution-feedback repair, under an unchanged validator, and
does the recovered data improve the specialist?

\subsection{Pilot: which repairer}

\input{generated/repair_pilot.tex}

\begin{observation}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Base as its own repairer}: recovered 7 of 237 rejected pairs (+17.1\%), below the pre-set +30\%, so the
pilot was no-go. Base copied the failed code back in 85 of 169 Scala repairs and 42 of 158 SQL repairs, even after 3
samples at $T=0.7$. 49 of 60 always-empty SQL programs stayed empty after repair.
\item \textbf{Instruct as repairer}: recovered 23 (+56.1\%) and wrote new code in 314 of 327 repairs (base: 200 of
327).
\item \textbf{Hand check} of all 23, each against its full table: 21 correct, 2 doubtful, 0 wrong (91.3\%), so go. The
doubtful cases were \texttt{gt\_08669} (the maximum of random string IDs; the question is ill-posed) and
\texttt{gt\_02705} (``average dwarf star mass'' answered with the column \texttt{mdeg/keV}).
\item 18 of the 23 were recovered by repairing the Scala, so the SQL training label is TS's own original SQL.
\end{itemize}
\end{observation}

\subsection{Recovery in the full runs}

\input{generated/repair.tex}

\begin{observation}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Base}: repair adds +48.2\% to +55.9\% kept pairs in round~1, and 18--25\% in rounds 2--3.
\item \textbf{Instruct}: it adds +15--23\% in every round.
\item On both models, most recoveries are \texttt{disagree} pairs (\cref{tab:gen-repair}).
\item \textbf{Which side is rewritten.} In round~1 most recovered pairs pass with a new Scala program and the
original SQL: base 147--172 of 204--224, Instruct 46--53 of 65--75. In rounds 2--3, a new SQL program is about as
common. Over all nine Instruct runs, 450 of 744 recovered pairs carry a repairer-written Scala program, 259 a
repairer-written SQL program and 35 both.
\item \texttt{always\_empty} pairs, the largest rejected group on Instruct (about 1,000 per round), are almost never
recovered: for example, 7 of 1,048 for Instruct seed~1, round~2.
\end{itemize}
\end{observation}

\section{Same-data control}\label{sec:ext-control}

\textbf{Question.} Is repair-TS's round-1 gain on base simply the effect of training on more pairs?

\input{generated/samedata.tex}

\begin{observation}
\textbf{Base}, \Mviii{} over 3 seeds: TS round~1 0.5266, control 0.5385, repair-TS round~1 0.5509.
\begin{itemize}
\item Control against TS: $+0.0119$ [$+0.0075$, $+0.0163$], $p<0.0001$. Seeds: $+0.0214$, $+0.0296$, $-0.0153$.
\item Repair-TS against control: $+0.0124$ [$+0.0079$, $+0.0170$], $p<0.0001$. Seeds: $+0.0067$, $+0.0130$,
$+0.0176$, all positive.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Instruct}: TS round~1 0.6152, control 0.6231, repair-TS round~1 0.6117.
\begin{itemize}
\item Control against TS: $+0.0079$, $p=0.0006$, all seeds positive.
\item Repair-TS against control: $-0.0114$, $p<0.0001$; 2 of 3 seeds negative.
\end{itemize}
\end{observation}

\begin{interpretation}
On base, the repair gain splits about evenly between \emph{more} data and \emph{different} data. The repaired pairs are
worth more than the same number of extra first-pass pairs, on every seed.

On Instruct the opposite holds. Extra first-pass pairs help, while repaired pairs are worth less than first-pass pairs.
Repair-TS's value on Instruct is therefore at most its data volume.

One caveat applies to base: the repaired pairs were written by a stronger model (Instruct). ``Different data'' may mean
\emph{better-written} data rather than \emph{repaired} data, a distillation effect. The control does not separate the
two (\cref{ch:discussion}).
\end{interpretation}

\section{Execution-based self-consistency}\label{sec:ext-sc}

\input{generated/sc_dev.tex}
\input{generated/sc_test.tex}

\begin{observation}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Temperature on dev.} $T=1.0$ beat $T=0.5$ and $T=0.7$ (dev mean 0.6520 against greedy 0.6225).
\item \textbf{Every model gains on the test sets} (\cref{tab:gen-sc-test}), by $+0.0141$ to $+0.0248$ \Mviii{}, all
$p<0.0001$ (\cref{tab:gen-significance}).
\item \textbf{Answers changed by the vote}, summed over cells, wrong$\to$right against right$\to$wrong:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item base: vanilla 278 / 82, TS 462 / 109, repair-TS 514 / 134;
  \item Instruct: vanilla 171 / 36, TS 504 / 98.
  \end{itemize}
\item Only 2 of 56 base cells and 2 of 32 Instruct cells fall.
\item The largest gains are on WikiSQL ($+0.03$ to $+0.08$), the smallest on Spider.
\item With the vote, the zero-shot means of 4 are: base repair-TS 0.6067, Instruct TS 0.6277, Instruct repair-TS
0.6300. All are above the paper's 0.5983.
\item Instruct repair-TS + vote against Instruct TS + vote: $-0.0000$ [$-0.0045$, $+0.0046$], $p=0.997$, a tie, with a
smaller seed spread (SD 0.0044 against 0.0086 \Mviii{}).
\end{itemize}
\end{observation}

\begin{interpretation}
Self-consistency is an established method~\cite{wang2022selfconsistency}, so this confirms rather than discovers. It is
useful here as a reference: TS's gain over vanilla (base, \Mviii{} $+0.0647$) is about 2.6 times the vote's gain on
vanilla ($+0.0248$), at no extra cost at test time. The vote costs 9 generations per case.
\end{interpretation}

\section{Few-shot vanilla against zero-shot Table-Specialist}\label{sec:ext-fewshot}

\begin{observation}
On Instruct, the repository's few-shot prompt with the \emph{vanilla} model gives a mean of 4 of 0.6090 (vote: 0.6341).
That is as high as TS round~3 with the zero-shot prompt (0.6113; vote: 0.6277). On base the corresponding figures are
0.5493 (few-shot vanilla) against 0.5460 (zero-shot TS round~3).
\end{observation}

\begin{interpretation}
Table-Specialist's gain is a \emph{zero-shot} gain. Fine-tuning mainly teaches the model to answer the zero-shot
prompt as well as the few-shot prompt already lets it answer. The paper does not state its test prompt. If its
Table~3 were few-shot, its vanilla scores would likely be higher than the ones it reports (as ours are), so Table~3 is
most consistent with a zero-shot protocol. This is a reading of the evidence, not a confirmation.
\end{interpretation}
