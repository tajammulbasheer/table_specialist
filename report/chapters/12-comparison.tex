\section{What explains the differences}

For each difference, the candidate causes below are listed with the evidence for each.

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.2em]
\item[Base vanilla far below the paper (WikiSQL $-0.199$).]
\emph{Most likely cause:} the model and query path. Instruct through the repository's chat call closes the gap on
three sets, while base fails under six settings. \emph{Not the cause:} WikiSQL letter case, which was tested and makes
it worse.
\item[Spider above the paper for every model (+0.055 to +0.121).]
\emph{Most likely cause:} the case set or the scoring, because the offset is the same for vanilla and fine-tuned
models. Our count matches the paper's, but the paper's case list is unpublished. The corrected score moves Spider by at
most +0.028, so order-sensitivity explains only part of it.
\item[Instruct TS slightly below the paper on WikiSQL, WikiTQ and BIRD (0.006--0.032).]
\emph{Candidates:} training corpus, training settings and the round reported. Our gains over vanilla are 0.005--0.025
smaller than the paper's. That is within the spread between our own seeds and rounds (for example, WikiSQL SD 0.031
across seeds).
\item[Our Scala, R and error-detection TS well above the paper's figures.]
\emph{Unattributed.} Vanilla matches the paper, so the difference arises in fine-tuning. Candidates are the corpus, our
loop choices (who writes errors, who validates), the model and the round reported. Our design does not separate them.
(The paper's own averages, such as 0.343 for Scala, include Text2Analysis; over the four public sets its Scala TS is
0.375.)
\item[Repair-TS above TS.]
This is attributable to the method within our setting: a paired comparison, same questions and tables, with a
same-data control. It is \emph{not} a comparison with the paper, which has no repair.
\end{description}

\begin{keypoint}
We make no claim of superiority over the paper. Where our numbers are higher (Spider; Scala and error-detection TS),
the cause is unattributed or lies in the test protocol. Our methodological contribution is measured only against our
own reproduction of TS, under identical conditions.
\end{keypoint}

\section{Computational and implementation constraints}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CPU-bound validation.} Spark validation, at 40--46~s per program with 36 workers, took 2.0--5.1~h per
round, and became the bottleneck once several seeds ran at once.
\item \textbf{Two GPUs.} Training and serving had to share them with strict one-job-per-GPU rules. GPU pools and a
validation lock kept both GPUs busy while one seed validated.
\item \textbf{Same budget for every arm.} No-validation trains on about 6.6 times more data per round, so it was the
most GPU-expensive arm despite skipping validation.
\item \textbf{Repository gaps cost engineering time.} The missing comparator, the broken evaluator and the sandbox limit
cost engineering effort, but did not limit what could be run.
\end{itemize}
